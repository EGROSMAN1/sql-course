Capstone assignment · Pharma Industry

Pharmaceutical industry model — preparation and assignment structure

Two dashboard pages where every number comes from a measure you write from scratch, with automatic color indication for product tiers. Working on Pharma_PowerBI_Star.xlsx — a pure star schema: one fact table, 28,400 rows, seven dimensions and only seven relationships. 29 exercises, each on a separate page with a full solution. Note: the download and the practice are On the computer only — Power BI Desktop doesn't exist on phone.

Dataset download — Pharma_PowerBI_Star.xlsx 8 tables · 28,400 rows · 7 relationships · For download on computer only
98,172,830.75USD net revenue — the anchor number
28,400Rows in the fact table
7Dimensions · 7 relationships
14Measures to write

The data is synthetic. The drug names, manufacturers, pharmacies and doctors were generated artificially. Don't draw any conclusions from them about the real pharmaceutical industry — but the economic structure is realistic: brand-name drugs carry a high gross margin, and generics a thin one.

Before we start — building the model. Load the eight sheets, turn off the Autodetect and manually build the seven relationships by sheet Relationships: Every dimension connects directly to Fact_Sales, all of them One to Many in the direction Single from the dimension to the fact. There is not and will not be a dimension-to-dimension relationship. Mark the Dim_Date As a date table (Mark as date table on Full_Date).

The trap of this bundle — cancelled rows that carry unrealized revenue. in the column Fact_Sales[Order_Status] Four values: Dispensed, Refilled, Cancelled andReversed. The last two are rows that were canceled or returned — yet they still carry a full amount inNet_Revenue_USD, because that's how the operational system records them. 2,514 Such rows, that carry 8,834,061.88 USD that never made it into the register — 9.0% from the naive sum. A measure that sums the column without a status filter will show revenue that doesn't exist. Throughout the assignment we'll write both numbers explicitly to see the gap.

Matching brackets. Table[Column] = column · [measure] = square brackets without a table name. For each measure: right-click on Fact_Sales in the panel Data ← New measure ← the formula ← Enter. The name must be exactly as it appears in the exercise.

1.1 · Total Net Revenue

Create a measure named Total Net Revenue that summarizes the Fact_Sales[Net_Revenue_USD]. Format with a thousands separator and two decimal digits.

Total Net Revenue =
SUM ( Fact_Sales[Net_Revenue_USD] )
  1. creationRight-click on Fact_Sales ← New measure
  2. checkDrag onto an empty card to make sure the number appears

Why specifically Net and not Gross: the column Gross_Revenue_USD It's the list price times the quantity, before discounts. Net_Revenue_USD It's the same column minus Discount_USD — meaning what was actually charged. The gap between the two in this file is 7,465,587.96 USD.

result: 98,172,830.75 USD. This is the naive number — we'll return to it in exercise 1.7.

1.2 · Total Units Dispensed

Create a second measure named Total Units Dispensed that summarizes the Fact_Sales[Units_Dispensed]. Note that this is an entirely different column from exercise 1.1.

Total Units Dispensed =
SUM ( Fact_Sales[Units_Dispensed] )

Two columns, two worlds: Net_Revenue_USD it's money andUnits_Dispensed It's a physical quantity. Both are fully additive — breaking either of them down by any of the seven dimensions sums up exactly to the total. Later we'll discover that the product with the most units isn't the product with the most money, and that's exactly the point.

result: 1,096,897 units.

1.3 · Active Products

Create a measure named Active Products that counts products unique based on Fact_Sales[Product_ID].

Active Products =
DISTINCTCOUNT ( Fact_Sales[Product_ID] )

Why not COUNT: COUNT ( Fact_Sales[Product_ID] ) would return 28,400 — it counts rows that have a value, not unique values. Every product appears here, on average, in236.7 Sales rows.

result: 120 products — exactly all the rows in Dim_Product, meaning there's no product in the catalog that never sold.

1.4 · Prescribers Engaged

Create a second DISTINCTCOUNT measure named Prescribers Engaged — this time on Fact_Sales[Prescriber_ID]. A different column, a different question.

Prescribers Engaged =
DISTINCTCOUNT (
    Fact_Sales[Prescriber_ID]
)

What question does the measure answer: 1.3 asks "how many products moved", and 1.4 asks "how many doctors prescribed at all". Two distinct numbers, two different columns in the same fact table. Note that this measure changes with every slice: with a slicer on a single region you'll get a much smaller number, and the sum of the numbers across all regions no will be 300 — a distinct count is not additive.

result: 300 Doctors prescribe.

1.5 · Revenue per Unit

Create a measure named Revenue per Unit — strictly from the two existing measures, without the division sign / and without AVERAGE. Format to two decimal places.

Revenue per Unit =
DIVIDE (
    [Total Net Revenue],
    [Total Units Dispensed]
)

The Lego principle: A new measure is built from existing measures, not columns. That way a change to the revenue definition automatically rolls through to everything that depends on it. DIVIDE returns blank instead of an error when the denominator is zero — / would crash the visual.

Interpretation warning: This is a price per unit weighted, not an average of prices. Cheap generics take up 38.2% From the units but only 2.8% of the money, so they pull this number down.

result: 89.5 USD per unit.

1.6 · Oncology Revenue

Create a measure named Oncology Revenue that returns the net revenue of the oncology department only, even when the visual is sliced by something completely different.

Oncology Revenue =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Net Revenue],
    Dim_Product[Therapeutic_Area]
        = "Oncology"
)

What CALCULATE does here: It replaces the filter on Therapeutic_Area in its own filter. If the visual is already filtered to Cardiology — the measure will still return Oncology. This is the normal behavior of CALCULATE, and in the next exercise we'll see how to change it.

result: 47,694,982.99 USD — 48.58% of total revenue. Oncology is 18% roughly of the products but almost half the money.

1.7 · Realized Revenue

Create a measure named Realized Revenue that returns only revenue that was actually realized — meaning rows whose status Dispensed or Refilled. Use -KEEPFILTERS so that the measure will honor the status slicer the user chose, and won't override it.

Realized Revenue =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Net Revenue],
    KEEPFILTERS (
        Fact_Sales[Order_Status] IN
            { "Dispensed", "Refilled" }
    )
)

Why KEEPFILTERS and not regular CALCULATE: without KEEPFILTERS, if the user selects in the slicer Cancelled only — the measure will override the selection and still return the sum of Dispensed andRefilled. The user will see a large number under the "Canceled" heading and won't understand. KEEPFILTERS Intersects the two filters with each other instead of replacing: choosing Cancelled will give blank, and that's the correct answer.

The gap versus 1.1: 98,172,830.75 less 89,338,768.87 they 8,834,061.88 USD of unrealized revenue — 9.0% than the naive number.

result: 89,338,768.87 USD realized, out of 98,172,830.75 naive.

1.8 · Pct of All Products

Create a measure named Pct of All Products that returns, for each product, its percentage share of the total revenue of all the rows inDim_Product — meaning a denominator that doesn't move as the row changes. Format as a percentage.

Pct of All Products =
DIVIDE (
    [Total Net Revenue],
    CALCULATE (
        [Total Net Revenue],
        ALL ( Dim_Product )
    )
)

What ALL does: It removes all filters from the dimension Dim_Product — not just from the name column, but also from the treatment field, the shape, and-Is_Generic. The denominator becomes constant, while the numerator keeps changing row by row.

The sanity test: in the table by Product_Name The Total row must show exactly 100.00%. If it shows less — you forgot ALL somewhere.

Result: the leader Endomycin = 7.36% · Total row = 100.00%.

1.9 · Revenue vs Target

Create a text measure named Revenue vs Target That compares each product to a target constant of 750,000 USD and returns Above Target or Below Target.

Revenue vs Target =
IF (
    [Total Net Revenue] >= 750000,
    "Above Target",
    "Below Target"
)

A fixed target vs. a calculated target: Here the target is a number hard-coded in the code. That’s intentional — a business target is set in advance and doesn’t move when the data moves. For comparison, the average revenue per product in the file is 818,106.92 USD, close to the target but not identical to it.

result: 35 Products above target · 85 beneath it.

1.10 · Product Tier — SWITCH

Create a measure named Product Tier that splits each product into three tiers by net revenue: Top from1,500,000 and above, Mid from400,000 and above, and everything else Low. Use -SWITCH ( TRUE () ), not in a nested IF.

Product Tier =
VAR R = [Total Net Revenue]
RETURN
SWITCH (
    TRUE (),
    R >= 1500000, "Top",
    R >= 400000, "Mid",
    "Low"
)

why VAR: without the variable, [Total Net Revenue] It was calculated twice in every row. With VAR it's calculated once, and the code also reads better.

The order is critical: SWITCH stops at the first condition that's met. If you swap the two rows, a product of 2,000,000 will get Mid — because it too is greater than400,000, and this condition is checked first.

result: 22 Top · 31 Mid · 67 Low — total 120 products.

1.11 · Tier Color — SWITCH on a value

Create a measure named Tier Color that translates every layer into a color code. This is SWITCH of the second kind — not TRUE () but a direct match to a value.

Tier Color =
SWITCH (
    [Product Tier],
    "Top", "#132449",
    "Mid", "#F5C518",
    "Low", "#A9B4CC"
)

Two faces of SWITCH: In 1.10 the first argument was TRUE () and every pair is condition and result. Here the first argument is an expression, and each pair is value and result. The same function, two usage forms.

note: This measure is never shown to the user. It exists to feed conditional formatting — we'll use it in exercise 3.2.

The three values: #132449 · #F5C518 · #A9B4CC.

1.12 · Product Rank — dense RANK

Create a measure named Product Rank that ranks products by net revenue from highest to lowest, in a ranking crowded (DENSE) — meaning without skipping numbers after a tie.

Product Rank =
RANK (
    DENSE,
    ALLSELECTED (
        Dim_Product[Product_Name]
    ),
    ORDERBY (
        [Total Net Revenue], DESC
    )
)

DENSE vs SKIP: If two products share place 3, DENSE would give the one after them 4, andSKIP will give it 5. In the ranking shown to the user DENSE Almost always what you want.

Why ALLSELECTED and not ALL: ALLSELECTED respects slicers. If the user filtered to Oncology, the ranking is rebuilt within that group and starts from 1 — which is what a user expects to see. With ALL The ranking would have stayed global and the table would have started at place 7 or 12.

result: Endomycin = 1 · Branpril = 120 (the last place among 120).

1.13 · Leader Revenue — INDEX

Create a measure named Leader Revenue that returns, in each row, the revenue of the product the leader — the exact same number in every row of the table.

Leader Revenue =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Net Revenue],
    INDEX (
        1,
        ALLSELECTED (
            Dim_Product[Product_Name]
        ),
        ORDERBY (
            [Total Net Revenue], DESC
        )
    )
)

What INDEX returns: One row out of a sorted list. 1 = the first, -1 = the last one. Here the sort is descending by revenue, so the first row is the top one.

Why this is useful: Once you have the leader's value in every row, you can write the percentage gap from the leader in a single line: DIVIDE ( [Total Net Revenue], [Leader Revenue] ).

result: 7,230,122.08 USD in every row — the revenue of Endomycin.

1.14 · Rolling 3M — WINDOW

Create a measure named Rolling 3M Revenue — A three-month moving average: the current month and the two months before it.

Rolling 3M Revenue =
AVERAGEX (
    WINDOW (
        -2, REL, 0, REL,
        ALLSELECTED (
            Dim_Date[Month_Key],
            Dim_Date[Month_Year]
        ),
        ORDERBY (
            Dim_Date[Month_Key], ASC
        )
    ),
    [Total Net Revenue]
)

To read the arguments: -2, REL = two rows back relative to the current row · 0, REL = up to the current row itself. Together: a window of three rows. AVERAGEX Runs the measure on each one and returns an average.

Why sort by Month_Key and not by Month_Year: Month_Year it's text — Apr-2025 import before Jan-2026 in the alphabet and will ruin the window. Month_Key It's an integer in the format YYYYMM, so its sort order is chronological. Both columns must be in the table the window is built on.

The first two rows: They don't have three months back, so they return the average of one and two rows respectively. This is correct behavior, not a bug.

result: Mar-2024 = 3,870,749.35 · Jun-2026 = 2,876,205.93 — over 30 months.

1.15 · Why the naive number is misleading

In this exercise, you don't write a measure. The CFO looks at the card you built in 1.1 and sees 98,172,830.75 USD. He asks you: "Is this the money that came in over the last two and a half years?" Explain — in your own words, before you open the solution — why the answer "yes" is wrong, why the mistake isn't caught by any consistency check, and what you would tell him instead.

the answer. the number 98,172,830.75 summarized the Net_Revenue_USD on all 28,400 The rows — including 1,730 rows Cancelled and 784 rows Reversed. Such rows were recorded in the operational system with the full amount, because the amount is set at order time, not at delivery time. They carry 8,834,061.88 USD that never made it into the register.

Why it doesn't look wrong in the visual. The column is fully additive: every breakdown of it — by channel, by region, by month — sums up to exactly the same total. No consistency check will flag it. The error isn't arithmetic, it's definitional: the column answers the question "how much money was recorded", and the user read it as though it answers "how much money came in".

What each of the two numbers actually says
the measureWhat it countsthe result
Total Net RevenueEvery row that was recorded, including cancelled and returned98,172,830.75
Realized RevenueOnly Dispensed and Refilled89,338,768.87
the gapRecorded revenue that is unrealized8,834,061.88 (9.0%)

The gap isn't uniform, and that's the dangerous part. If the cancellations were spread evenly, the naive number would be inflated by the same 9.0% everywhere and the rankings would remain correct. In practice they're not evenly distributed: the leader Endomycin descending from7,230,122.08 to 6,671,042.61 When filtering to the fulfilled status, whereas Renanavir descending from6,893,688.31 to6,261,554.95 — a smaller drop in percentage. Every ranking, every percentage, and every "who is leading" is rebuilt from scratch.

What actually happens. Three options, in order of preference: (1) measure Realized Revenue with KEEPFILTERS As we wrote in 1.7, and we also leave the naive number in the dashboard under an explicit name like Booked Revenue; (2) a report-level filter on Order_Status — simple, but hides the information about cancellations entirely; (3) filtering in Power Query — faster, but the rows are deleted from the model and you can no longer analyze the cancellations themselves. Don't choose (3) before making sure no one needs to count cancellations.

the gap: 8,834,061.88 USD — 9.0% than the naive number.

1. The largest channel isn't the most profitable. Retail Counter brings 40.68% of revenue — more than any other channel — but its margin percentage 73.91%, lower than that of Specialty Clinic that stands at 75.5%. Every shekel that passes through the specialist clinic generates more profit than a shekel that passes through the counter.

2. The tail is the problem, not the head. Wholesale Direct It is the smallest of the channels (8.47% of revenue) and also the least profitable (70.48%). The product of the two weaknesses is what makes it the first candidate to check — not its size alone and not its percentage alone.

3. Where the gap comes from. The explanation isn't in the channel but in the mix flowing through it. Specialty Clinic carries the expensive drugs with the high margin — oncology and immunology. Wholesale Direct Topic: a large volume of cheap generics, where the cost is 58% until 78% from the price. The channel isn't "less efficient"; it simply sells different products. That's the distinction that separates a correct conclusion from an expensive decision.

4. What does that say about the decision. "Close Wholesale Direct" is the hasty conclusion. The channel generates 5,863,920.93 USD gross margin in practice, and most of its costs are variable, not fixed. The right question is whether you can shift toward it a different product mix — not whether to cut it.

The gap between the columns will be much larger than the gap between the rows — and that's the proof that the mix, not the channel, is what explains the profitability.
