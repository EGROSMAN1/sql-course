Chapter I · The VertiPaq engine

What is VertiPaq

Tabular storage engine In-Memory column-based — the core on which Power BI's data model is built.

1

In-Memory

The engine runs entirely in RAM and doesn't access disk during the calculation.

2

Column-based

Stores the data by columns, unlike SQL which stores by rows — enabling efficient compression and retrieval.

3

Core technology

responsible for storage, compression and data retrieval — and it's the same engine that also runs Analysis Services.

Chapter I · The VertiPaq engine

What VertiPaq actually does

Four roles, all on data compressed in memory.

1

Data compression

Identifies repeating values and builds internal dictionaries. Compression ratios of 1:10 are routine.

2

Columnar storage

measure on Sales Amount touches only one column — the other 14 columns aren't read at all.

3

DAX processing

Calculates in real time on the compressed data and returns a result to the visual.

4

Managing reciprocal relationships

Translates a filter from the dimension table to the fact table — and that's exactly the topic of the lesson.

Chapter I · The VertiPaq engine

Why an engine like this is even needed

1

Data volumes

Millions of rows on a regular laptop, without draining memory.

2

An interactive experience

Clicking a filter returns a result immediately, with no waiting.

3

Quick calculations

Efficient scanning while computing measures — critical in complex models.

The point that will accompany the whole lesson: The engine is fast, but only if the model is built correctly. A flat or branched model cancels out the advantage.

Chapter II · Why relationships are needed

Delete the relationships — and the star falls apart

  • Delete all the relationships in Model view — and the star goes back to being seven islands.
  • There's no pipe through which a filter can flow from the dimension to Sales: any filtering is simply ignored.
  • Every visual in the report shows the same number — $109,809,274.20 — no matter what you select.
  • The reason: the measure runs over the whole table; without a relationship there's nothing to narrow the Filter Context.

Without relationships there's no model — just tables that don't talk to each other. The relationship is what turns data into a report.

Chapter II · Why relationships are needed

A relationship = a pipe through which the filter flows

  • A relationship connects a column in one table to a matching column in another.
  • It doesn't copy data — it opens a pipe through which filters flow.
  • when choosing Category = Bikes, the filter flows through the pipe and narrows Sales.
  • The natural flow direction: from the dimension (the descriptor) to the Fact (the events).
  • Every relationship has three properties: cardinality, directionality, and active or inactive.

Chapter IV · Star vs. Snowflake

Why not one giant table

  • duplicate: The product name, category and color used to repeat across all 121,253 rows.
  • maintenance: Renaming a category = updating tens of thousands of rows.
  • analysis: You can't see products that weren't sold — they simply don't exist in the table.

in our file 47 out of 397 products were never sold, and 66 out of 702 marketers with no sales. In a single flat table this information disappears.

The solution: split by responsibility. One table records what happened, other tables describe who’s involved. Less memory, more speed, and full analysis.

Chapter IV · Star vs. Snowflake

Snowflake — dimensions split into a chain

  • Same Fact at the center — but the dimensions are normalized (split) into tables.
  • instead Dim Product One wide table: Category ← Subcategory ← Product.
  • Every filter on Category passes through a chain of three relationships on the way to Fact.
  • Comes from the world of data warehouses (DWH), where normalization saves disk space.
  • The name: the diagram branches out like a snowflake.

Chapter IV · Star vs. Snowflake

So why does Star win

  • In a traditional DWH, normalization saves disk space — a real advantage.
  • But VertiPaq compresses columns brilliantly, and the compression cancels out the savings.
  • What's left of Snowflake is only the drawbacks: more relationships, slower performance, complex DAX.

The practical rule: In Power Query you merge the chain of dimensions into one wide dimension table. In our file this is already done — Category and Subcategory sit as columns inside Product.

Chapter V · The relationship mechanism

Cardinality

  • Cardinality = the numeric ratio between two tables in a relationship.
  • One-to-Many (1:N) — a unique dimension filters a repeating Fact. That's the healthy state.
  • Example: one product (1) sold across thousands of sales rows (N).
  • Many-to-Many (N:M) — both tables have duplicates. A warning sign.
  • The marker on the line: 1 on the dimension side, * On the Fact side.

If Power BI suggests N:M — stop. In most cases a duplicate got into the dimension table, and that's what needs fixing.

Chapter V · The relationship mechanism

Filter directionality

Single — defaultBoth — bidirectional
The flow directionFrom the 1 side to the * side onlyAlso from Fact back to dimension
behaviorPredictable, fast, safeCan distort results with indirect filtering
performanceeasy to preventheavier
Additional risknoneAmbiguous filter paths (Ambiguity)

The analogy: Single = one-way street, traffic is predictable. Both = a roundabout with no traffic signs — sometimes it works out, and under load it crashes. In 99% of cases you leave it as Single.

Chapter V · The relationship mechanism

Active vs. inactive relationship

  • Sales has three date columns: OrderDateKey, DueDateKey, ShipDateKey.
  • They all want to connect to Date — but only One active relationship Between a pair of tables.
  • The active relationship (solid line): OrderDateKey — the default for all calculations.
  • The inactive relationships (dashed line): lie ready and are activated selectively in DAX.

How do you activate an inactive relationship? Point-in-time, within a specific calculation — and only for that calculation. The active relationship and the rest of the report are unaffected. We'll learn the exact syntax in the DAX lesson; at this stage the important thing is understanding the mechanism: the relationship exists, lying ready, and gets activated on demand.

Chapter V · The relationship mechanism

Why an inactive relationship is actually good

?

Why only one is active

Two active relationships at the same time = means ambiguity. Power BI wouldn't know which date to filter by, so it forbids this.

✓

What we gained

One clean date table serves all three dates, instead of duplicating it three times.

In AdventureWorks there are 2,113 rows with no ship date ($1,281,073.41) — on the ShipDateKey relationship they'll fall into the blank row.

Chapter VI · Bidirectional relationships in depth

Why not just a slicer on Customer

"Why not just put Customer in the slicer too?" Because a slicer is what you we know — and the list you're looking for is exactly what you no know.

Slicer = input

On Product it's easy: you know you want "sunglasses" — you click and select. But what would you pick in the Customer slicer? You don't know which of the 2,000 customers bought sunglasses — that's exactly the answer you're looking for.

The list = the output

The manager says: "Pull a list of everyone who bought sunglasses, for an SMS campaign". The list is the result, not the selection. Both lets the 813 sales rows tell Customer who appears in them — and the list narrows down to 662 on its own.

and the opposite case — the Both crosses a side

\"What did Mia Dubois buy?\" — now we know the customer and are looking for products. Same idea, opposite direction.

Mia DuboisSlicer · Customer

the rule: What you know ← goes to the slicer. What you're looking for ← is where the filtering needs to reach. And if along the way it has to climb from the fact table to a dimension — for that, and only that, Both.

Why does it sometimes "work" even without Both? If the values in the matrix come from Sales (say, sum of sales), empty rows are automatically hidden and everything looks fine. But the moment the matrix relies on the dimension itself — a list of names, a customer count, fields from Customer only — without Both you'll get all 2,000 rows.

When yes and when no

✓ Turn on Both when…

The question is "which / who from the dimension?" and the filter sits in a different dimension: which customers bought X, which products did customer Y buy, which employees sold in category Z. The telltale sign: a list that refuses to shrink — 2,000 instead of 662.

✗ Don't turn on Both when…

The question is "how much?" — sums, quantities, trends. Single is always enough there. And don't turn on "just in case" for everything: every extra bi-directional pipe complicates the model and slows it down.

The sentence to remember: Filtering always flows with the arrow, from the dimension to the fact. Both doesn't change the relationship — it just adds a second arrowhead, so the fact can filter the dimension back. You open it only for a "which / who" question, and close it when you're done.

VertiPaq

What does a relationship between two tables in the model actually do?

explanation: without a relationship, each table lives on its own. Power BI does guess some relationships on load, but not always correctly.

In a sales-by-category visual, with no relationship between the products table and the sales table — what would show?

What's the natural direction of filter flow in a star schema?

explanation: The dimension describes, the fact records. Filtering flows down from the descriptor to the events — that is the arrow direction.

In Model view, what does the arrow on the relationship line signify?

explanation: The arrow is the filter direction. Filtering against the direction of the arrow simply doesn't happen.

A model where every dimension connects directly to the fact table — how many "hops" does a filter take from the dimension to the data?

explanation: This is the advantage of a star schema: one direct relationship between each dimension and the fact.

In a model where the dimensions are split into a chain (category ← subcategory ← product), what happens to a filter on the category?

Where does the primary key sit in a valid relationship?

explanation: The 1-side is the dimension: one value = exactly one row, no duplicates and no blanks.

explanation: The * side is the fact. The same key value appears in many event rows.

What do the symbols 1 and * on the relationship line indicate?

Two tables with the same row count and the same grain — for example a sales row and an order row. What relationship will be created?

explanation: A 1:1 relationship is perfectly valid, and it's a sign that the tables describe the exact same event.

Power BI suggests a many-to-many cardinality between dimension and fact. What's the first step?

explanation: Many-to-many between a dimension and a fact is almost always a symptom of duplication in the dimension, not a solution.

Why is a many-to-many relationship considered riskier than one-to-many?

explanation: When both sides have duplicates, there's no single way to match row to row — and the result is surprising.

A sales table with 121,253 rows is linked to a customers table with 18,485 rows. Where does the * sit?

explanation: The repeating side is always the fact — every customer appears in many sales rows.

explanation: A relationship is built between columns of the same type. A mismatch prevents the relationship from being created in the first place.

explanation: One-directional is predictable, fast, and safe, and suits the vast majority of cases.

You selected a category in the slicer, and the matrix showing customer names didn't shrink at all. What's the likely reason?

explanation: This is normal behavior for a single-direction relationship: filtering flows only with the arrow.
